\chapter{Model Predictive Current Control}

\section{Prediction Model}
This section discusses the derivation of the nonlinear, discrete-time prediction of the PMSM’s dynamics. First, the decision-making process for this prediction model is outlined in the context of field-oriented current control for PMSMs. Next, the process of discretizing the continuous-time system is explained. For the current controller in this chapter, this is relatively simple; however, it poses greater challenges in the observer-based current prediction controller in Chapter \ref{ch:ipred}. Finally, as is typical for MPC, the prediction is extended to the entire prediction horizon. The assumptions relevant to the prediction are also presented there.
\subsection{Choice of adequate State Space Model}
As derived in Section \ref{sec:model_pmsm}, the model of the interior magnet PMSM is given by Equation \eqref{equ:2_apv_ss}.
\begin{equation}
	\underbrace{
	\dfrac{\diff}{\diff t} \begin{bmatrix}
		i_{Sd} \\i_{Sq}
	\end{bmatrix}}_{\Dot{\x}_c}
	= 
	\underbrace{
	\begin{bmatrix}
		-\frac{R_S}{L_d} & \frac{L_q}{L_d} \omega(t)\\ -\frac{L_d}{L_q} \omega(t) & -\frac{R_S}{L_q}
	\end{bmatrix}}_{\bm{A}_c(\omega(t))}
	\underbrace{
	\begin{bmatrix}
		i_{Sd} \\i_{Sq}
	\end{bmatrix}}_{\x_c}
 + 
 	\underbrace{
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\frac{1}{L_d} & 0 \\ 0 & \frac{1}{L_q}
	\end{bmatrix}}_{\bm{B}_c}
	\underbrace{
	\begin{bmatrix}
		u_{Sd} \\u_{Sq}
	\end{bmatrix}}_{\bm{u}_c}
 +
 \underbrace{ 
 \begin{bmatrix}
		0 \\ - \frac{\psi_{PM}}{L_q} \omega(t)
	\end{bmatrix}}_{\bm{E}_c(\omega(t))}
	\label{equ:2_apv_ss}
\end{equation}
For the ease of notation, $\omega = \omega_e = \omega_{m1}p$ refers to the electrical rotor speed for the rest of this thesis. Writing the nonlinear continuous time dynamic system in established control theory\cite{RegelTech} \cite{mpcRawlings} notation leads to the following :
\begin{equation}
	\begin{split}
		\Dot{\x}_c  &= \bm{f}(\bm{x}_c,\bm{u}_c,\omega )\\
		\Dot{\x}_c &= \bm{A}_c(\omega(t)) \x_c + \bm{B}_c {\bm{u}_c} + \bm{E}_c(\omega(t))
	\end{split}
	\label{equ:2_apv_cs_ss}
\end{equation}



%or in compact notation:
%\[
%\Sigma: \left(\bm{A}_c(\omega(t)),\bm{B}_c,\bm{E}(\omega(t))\right)
%\]
For a variety of applications it is beneficial to work with linear models, since this allows for the use of broad framework of control schemes. However, the affine term regarding the induced voltage $\bm{E}_c(\omega(t))$ is non-negligable for the current dynamics $\Dot{\x}$ at every operating point except standstill or locked rotor operation.\\

As mentioned in Section \reminder{$ref{sec:intro_comp_cs}$}, using model predictive control, one can directly use a MIMO formulation, leading to a direct consideration the D/Q axis coupling. The question remaining is whether a discretized model of Equation \eqref{equ:2_apv_ss} shall be directly used or a step-wise linearization approach around an operating point shall be employed. For the linearization, one would define an operating point as
\begin{equation*}
	\bm{x}_c
	=
	\bm{x}_{c,\mathrm{OP}}
	+
	\bm{\delta x}_c,
	\qquad
	\bm{u}_c
	=
	\bm{u}_{c,\mathrm{OP}}
	+
	\bm{\delta u}_c,
	\qquad
	\omega
	=
	\omega_{\mathrm{OP}}
	+
	\delta\omega .
\end{equation*}

And approximate the dynamics \eqref{equ:2_apv_cs_ss} using a first-order Taylor approximation around an operating point $\mrm{OP} = (\bm{x}_{c,\mathrm{OP}},\bm{u}_{c,\mathrm{OP}},\omega_{\mathrm{OP}})$
\begin{align*}
	\frac{\diff \bm{\delta x}_c}{\diff t}
	&=
	\left.
	\frac{\partial\bm{f}}{\partial\bm{x}_c}
	\right|_{\mathrm{OP}}
	\bm{\delta x}_c
	+
	\left.
	\frac{\partial\bm{f}}{\partial\bm{u}_c}
	\right|_{\mathrm{OP}}
	\bm{\delta u}_c
	+
	\left.
	\frac{\partial\bm{f}}{\partial\omega}
	\right|_{\mathrm{OP}}
	\delta\omega .
\end{align*}
Analyzing the nonlinear system \eqref{equ:2_apv_ss}, it can be seen that th only non-linearity stems from the induced voltage  $\bm{E}_c(\omega(t))$ , which is proportional to the scheduling parameter $\omega$. As shown in Section \ref{sec:modelling}, the dynamics of the mechanical system are slower than the electrical dynamics, assuming $\omega(t)$ is slowly varying relative to the sampling period $T_S$. Therefore, introducing step-wise linearization only leads to increased complexity without reducing the computational effort.\\


Thus, it was decided to use the affine prediction model of Equation \eqref{equ:2_apv_ss} directly. This approach is also typically used in modern MPC PMSM literature \cite{TOMPFC1} \cite{XuchenPMSMiObs} \cite{ofmpcDistObs}. %TODO: cite  noch viele andere

\subsection{Discretization}
Due to its simplicity, Forward Euler discretization shall be considered. This approach conserves stability, when the sampling time $T_S$ is sufficiently small. The mapping of continuous time eigenvalues to discrete time eigenvalues $\underline{s}_i = a + \J b \; : \; a,b \in \mathbb{R} \rightarrow \underline{z}_i$ is described as
\[
\underline{z}_i = 1 + T_S \underline{s}_i
\]
For $\underline{z}_i$ to be within the unit circle
\[
\| \underline{z}_i \| = \| 1 + T_S \underline{s}_i \| = 1 + 2T_S^2 a + T_S a^2  + T_S b^2 < 1 \iff  T_S^2 (a^2 + b^2) < - 2 T_S a
\]
must hold. So, for positive $T_S$, this can be rewritten as
\[
T_S \| \underline{s}_i \|^2 < -2 \RE\{\underline{s}_i\} \iff T_S < \frac{ -2 \RE\{\underline{s}_i\}}{ \| \underline{s}_i \|^2}
\]
With the eigenvalue analysis of Section \ref{sec:modelling} and $T_S \le \qty{200}{\micro s}$ within the scope of this thesis, applying the Forward Euler approach will lead to stable discretization for the model of the PMSM. The discretized state space model is then obtained by:
\begin{align*}
	\boldsymbol{A}(\omega[k]) &= \boldsymbol{I} + T_S\boldsymbol{A}_c(\omega(kT_S)) \quad \boldsymbol{B} = T_S \boldsymbol{B}_c \quad \\  
	\boldsymbol{E}(\omega[k]) &= \boldsymbol{E}_c (\omega(kT_S)) 
\end{align*}
In literature the actuating signal is often split up as $ \bm{u}[k]=\bm{u}[k-1] + \bm{\Delta u}[k]$. This mainly offers benefits as it eases the stability analysis  \cite{mpcCamacho} \cite{mpcRawlings}. So, the discrete time-model for prediction is given by Equation \eqref{equ:2_apv_tk}.
\begin{equation}
	\begin{split}
		\x [k+1] &= \Aw \x[k] + \B (\bm{u}[k-1] + \bm{\Delta u}[k] ) + \Ew \\
%		\begin{bmatrix}i_{Sd}[k+1] \\ i_{Sq}[k+1] 
%		\end{bmatrix}  =
		\x[k+1] &= \begin{bmatrix} 
			1-\frac{T_s R_S}{L_d} & T_s\frac{L_q}{L_d}\omega[k] \\
			 -T_s\frac{L_d}{L_q}\omega[k] & 1-\frac{T_s R_S}{L_q} 
		\end{bmatrix} \x[k] \\
		 &\quad+ 
		 \begin{bmatrix}
		 	 \frac{T_s}{L_d} & 0 \\
		 	  0 & \frac{T_s}{L_q} 
		 \end{bmatrix} \left( \bm{u}[k-1]+\bm{\Delta u}[k] \right) + 
		 \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\
		 	 -\frac{T_s\psi_{PM}}{L_q}\omega[k] 
 		 \end{bmatrix}
	\end{split}
	\label{equ:2_apv_tk}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Prediction Horizon} \label{sec:prediction_horizon}
In model predictive control one distinguishes between the prediction horizon $N_P$, which describes how many discrete steps in time the dynamics are predicted and the control horizon $N_C$, which is the amount of optimal actuating signal are computed at every step (w)ith $N_C \le N_P$). % TODO: maybe move this to the intro
Within the scope of this thesis, typically field-oriented current control sampling times of $T_S \in [5,\, 20]\;\si{kHz}$ are considered. To get an optimal trade off between model fault tolerance, tracking performance and computational effort a typical prediction horizon of $N_P \le 3$  with $N_P = N_C$ are considered.\\
To obtain a prediction for  $\x[k+l]\; l\in \mathbb{N}\;:\; l\le N_P $ , Equation \eqref{equ:2_apv_tk} is iterated one step ahead.
\begin{align*}
	\xh[k+2] = \bm{A}(\hat{\omega}[k+1]) \xh[k+1] + \B (\bm{u}[k] + \bm{\Delta u}[k+1] ) + \bm{E}(\hat{\omega}[k+1])
\end{align*}
Using Relation \eqref{equ:2_apv_tk} for the estimation $\xh[k+1]$, $\bm{u}[k]  = \bm{u}[k-1] + \bm{\Delta u}[k] $ and assuming constant rotor speed for all prediction steps $\hat{\omega}[k+l] = \omega[k]$, leads to
\begin{align*}
	\xh[k+2] &= \Aw \left(\Aw \x[k] + \B (\bm{u}[k-1] + \bm{\Delta u}[k] + \Ew \right) \\ 
	&+  \B (\bm{u}[k-1] + \bm{\Delta u}[k] + \bm{\Delta u}[k+1]) + \Ew \\
	\xh[k+2] &= \left(\Aw \right)^2 + \left(\Aw + \I \right) \B \bm{u}[k-1] \\ & + \B  \left(\Aw + \I \right) \bm{\Delta u} [k] + \B \bm{\Delta u} [k+1] + \left(\Aw + \I \right) \Ew
\end{align*}
This continues up to $l=N_P$.
\begin{align*}
	\xh[k+N_P]
	&= \left(\Aw\right)^{N_P}\x[k]
	+ \left(\sum_{f=0}^{N_P-1}\left(\Aw\right)^f\right)
	\B\bm{u}[k-1] \\
	&\quad
	+ \sum_{h=0}^{N_P-1}
	\left(
	\sum_{f=0}^{N_P-1-h}
	\left(\Aw\right)^f
	\right)
	\B\bm{\Delta u}[k+h] \\
	&\quad
	+ \left(\sum_{f=0}^{N_P-1}\left(\Aw\right)^f\right)
	\Ew 
\end{align*}
For the nominal system \eqref{equ:2_apv_tk}, the output is equal to the current states $y[k] = x[k]$, but for other formulations with $\bm{C}  \ne \I$ it is necessary to multiply the state description from the left by $\bm{C}$ to obtain an estimation for the output $\hat{\bm{y}}[k+l]$. Doing that and using vector notations for all $l\in [1, N_P]$ of $\hat{\bm{y}}[k+l]$, leads to 

\begin{align}
\begin{split}
	&
\underbrace{
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\hat{\bm{y}}[k+1] \\
		\hat{\bm{y}}[k+2] \\
		\vdots \\
		\hat{\bm{y}}[k+N_P] \\
	\end{bmatrix}
}_{\Bar{\bm{y}}[k+1]} =
\underbrace{
	\begin{bmatrix}
	\bm{C} \Aw  \\
	\bm{C}  \left(\Aw \right)^2 \\
	\vdots \\
	\bm{C} \left(\Aw \right)^{N_P-1} \\
	\end{bmatrix}
}_{\bm{P}_x}
\x[k] +
\underbrace{
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\bm{C} \B \\
		\bm{C}\left(\Aw + \I \right) \B \\
		\vdots \\
		\bm{C} \left( \sum_{l=0}^{N_P-1} \left(\Aw \right)^l \right) \B 
	\end{bmatrix}
}_{\bm{P}_u}
\bm{u}[k-1] + \\
&
\underbrace{
	\begin{bmatrix}
%		\scriptstyle
		\bm{CB} & \bm0_2 & \cdots & \bm0_2\\
		\bm{C}(\bm{A}(\omega[k])+\bm{I})\bm{B} & \bm{CB} & \cdots & \bm0_2\\
		\bm{C}\left(\left(\bm{A}(\omega[k])\right)^2+\bm{A}(\omega[k])+\bm{I}\right)\bm{B} & \bm{C}(\bm{A}(\omega[k])+\bm{I})\bm{B}  & \ddots & \vdots\\
		\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \bm0_2\\
		\bm{C}\left(\sum\limits_{l=0}^{N_P-1}\left(\bm{A}(\omega[k])\right)^l\right)\bm{B}
		&
		\bm{C}\left(\sum\limits_{l=0}^{N_P-2}\left(\bm{A}(\omega[k])\right)^l\right)\bm{B}
		&
		\cdots
		&
		\bm{CB}
	\end{bmatrix}
}_{\predDeltaU} 
\underbrace{
	\begin{bmatrix}
%		\scriptstyle
		{ \boldsymbol{\Delta u}}[k] \\
		{ \boldsymbol{\Delta u}}[k+1]\\
		\vdots \\
		\vdots \\
		{ \boldsymbol{\Delta u}}[k+N_P-1]
	\end{bmatrix}
}_{\Deltaub} \\
&+ 
\underbrace{
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\bm{C} \\
		\bm{C}\left(\Aw + \I \right) \\
		\vdots \\
		\bm{C} \left( \sum_{l=0}^{N_P-1} \left(\Aw \right)^l \right) 
	\end{bmatrix}
}_{\bm{P}_E}
\Ew
\end{split}
\end{align}
\begin{equation}
	\Bar{\bm{y}}[k+1] = \underbrace{ \bm{P}_{x} \Bar{\x}[k] + \bm{P}_u \bm{u}[k-1] + \bm{P}_{E}\Ew }_{=:\Bar{\bm{g}}[k]} + \bm{P}_{\Bar{\Delta u}} \Deltaub
	\label{equ:2_prediction}
\end{equation}
With $\Bar{\bm{g}}[k]$ being parameter variant, but independent of $\Deltaub$. For the implementation it is beneficial to recognize that the columns of $\predDeltaU$ are down-shifted columns of $\bm{P}_u$.

\section{Optimization Problem}
The vector of the voltage increment at the current step in time and its predictions up to the $N_P$th step shall be determined optimal with respect to a certain cost function $J$ and constraints regarding the stator voltage and the predicted stator current response, such that the determined stator voltage can be physically applied without damaging the machine. For the actuating signal $\Bar{\bm{u}_S}[k]$, this means it has to be bounded by the voltage hexagon and the magnitude of each $\Bar{\bm{i}_S}[k+1]$ must not exceed the current limit $I_{max}$. Doing so, leads to a general description for MPC for PMSM, similar to the one used in \cite{TOMPFC1}.
\begin{align*}
	\Bar{\bm{\Delta u}_S}[k]^* = \arg \min_{\Bar{\bm{\Delta  u}_S}}\; &J(\boldsymbol{u}_{S}[k], \boldsymbol{i}_{S}[k], \boldsymbol{i}_{S,ref}[k+1])\\
	 \text{s.t.} \quad \hat{\boldsymbol{i}}_{S}[k+1] \in \mathbb{I}_S &= \{\boldsymbol{i}_{S} \in \mathbb{R}^2 :\|\boldsymbol{i}_{S}^R[k+1]\|_2 \leq {I}_{max} \} \\
	  \boldsymbol{u}_{S}^S[k] \in \mathbb{U}_{S}^S &= \{ \boldsymbol{u}_{S}^S \in \mathbb{R}^2 : \boldsymbol{u}_{S}^S = U_{DC} \boldsymbol{T}_{abc\rightarrow dq} \boldsymbol{d}_{abc} \}
\end{align*}
Since the controller operates within the RRF, the relation for the voltage constraints must be transformed. To gurantee a unique global minimizer, $J$ must be a convex function and $\mathbb{U}_{S}^R, \mathbb{I}_{S}^R$ convex sets, expressed as a set of linear inequality constraints. To use the framework of quadratic programming, the constraints must be expressed in terms of the optimization variable $\Deltaub$. Keeping this in mind and using index $u$ for quantities regarding the prediction vector of the actuating signal $\Bar{\bm{u}_S}[k]$ and $i$ for quantities regarding the output prediction vector $\Bar{\bm{i}_S}[k+1]$, the goal of this section is to derive a quadratic program in the form of
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
	\Bar{\bm{\Delta u}_S^R}[k]^* & = \arg \min_{\Bar{\bm{\Delta  u}_S}}\;   \frac{1}{2} \left(\Deltaubsr \right)^\T \bm{\Gamma} \left(\Deltaubsr \right) + \bm{\gamma}^\T \Deltaubsr\\
	& = \arg \min_{\Bar{\bm{\Delta  u}_S}}\;  \frac{1}{2} \| {\Deltaubsr} \|_{\bm{\Gamma}}^2 + \bm{\gamma}^\T \Deltaubsr \\
	\text{s.t.} & \quad \underbrace{
		\begin{bmatrix}
			\bm{M}_u \\
			\bm{M}_i
		\end{bmatrix}
	}_{\bm{M}}
	\Deltaubsr
	\le
	\underbrace{
		\begin{bmatrix}
			\bm{\mu}_u\\
			\bm{\mu}_i
	\end{bmatrix}}_{\bm{\mu}}
	\label{equ:2_QP}
\end{split}
\end{equation}
with the Hessian matrix of the cost function being positive definite $\nabla_{\Deltaub}^2 J = \bm{\Gamma}  \succ 0 $ to ensure convexity.

\subsection{Cost Function}
For the cost function $J$, a simple LQR-style base cost is employed. With $\bm{Q} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_P q\times N_P q}$ as the diagonal weighting matrix for the tracking error and $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_P m\times N_P m} $ the diagonal weighting matrix  for the stator voltage increment. For the application of electric drives, reference tracking is significantly more important then the magnitude of the stator voltage.
\begin{equation*}
J = \left(\; \bm{\Bar{i}} [k+1] - \boldsymbol{\Bar{i}}_{ref} [k+1] \; \right)^\T \boldsymbol{Q} \left(\; \boldsymbol{\Bar{i}} [k+1] - \boldsymbol{\Bar{i}}_{ref} [k+1]\; \right) + \left(\Deltaub \right)^\T \; \boldsymbol{R} \; \Deltaub
\end{equation*}
Or with the more compact $\mathcal{L}_2$-norm notation $\wLNorm{x}{I} = \x^\T \I \x$  :
\begin{equation}
	J = \| \bm{\Bar{i}}  - \bm{\Bar{i}}_{ref} \|_{Q}^2  + \wLNorm{\Bar{\boldsymbol{\Delta u}}}{R}
	\label{equ:2_J}
\end{equation}
To express the cost $J$ in terms of $\Deltaub$, the prediction for the current as output signal is expressed by the prediction based on the system $\Sigma: \left[\bm{A}(\omega[k]),\bm{B},\bm{E}(\omega[k])\right]$ according to Equation \eqref{equ:2_prediction}. 
\[
\Bar{\bm{i}}[k+1]  = \Bar{\bm{y}}[k+1] = \Bar{\bm{g}}[k] + \predDeltaU \Deltaub
\]
A prediction of the reference current is given as input $ \Bar{\bm i}_{ref} [k+1] = \Bar{\bm r} [k+1]$.
\begin{align*}
	\Bar{\bm{i}}[k+1] - \Bar{\bm{i}}_{ref}[k+1]
	&= \Bar{\bm{y}}[k+1] - \Bar{\bm{r}}[k+1] \\
	&= \underbrace{\bm{P}_x \x + \bm{P}_u \bm{u}[-1] + \bm{P}_E \Ew}_{\Bar{\bm{g}}[k]}
	- \Bar{\bm{r}}[k+1] + \predDeltaU \Deltaub \\
	&= \Bar{\Tilde{\bm{e}}}[k+1] + \predDeltaU \Deltaub
\end{align*}
Here, $\Tilde{\Bar{\bm{e}}}[k+1] = \Bar{\bm{g}}[k] - \Bar{\bm{r}}[k+1]$ is defined as the prediction of the quasi-tracking error. This is a summation of the prediction terms independent of $\Deltaub$. The separation is done in order to use $\Deltaub$ as the vector of optimization variables. Inserting this into $\eqref{equ:2_J}$, leads to:
\[
J = \left(\Bar{\Tilde{\bm{e}}}[k+1] + \predDeltaU \Deltaub \right)^T \bm{Q} \left(\Bar{\Tilde{\bm{e}}}[k+1] + \predDeltaU \Deltaub\right) +  \left(\Deltaub \right)^\T \; \boldsymbol{R} \; \Deltaub
\]
For readability the indices $k$ for the quasi-error prediction are not written down. Using the symmetric property of the diagonal weighting matrices $\bm{Q,R}$, the cost function is expanded to:

\begin{align*}
	J
	={}&
	\Bar{\Tilde{\bm{e}}}^{T}
	\bm{Q}
	\Bar{\Tilde{\bm{e}}}
	+
	2\left(\Deltaub\right)^T
	\predDeltaU^T
	\bm{Q}
	\Bar{\Tilde{\bm{e}}}
	\\
	&+
	\left(\Deltaub\right)^T
	\predDeltaU^T
	\bm{Q}
	\predDeltaU
	\Deltaub
	+
	\left(\Deltaub\right)^T
	\boldsymbol{R}
	\Deltaub
	\\
	={}&
	\left(\Deltaub\right)^T
	\left(
	\predDeltaU^T
	\bm{Q}
	\predDeltaU
	+
	\boldsymbol{R}
	\right)
	\Deltaub
	+
	2
	\Bar{\Tilde{\bm{e}}}^{T}
	\bm{Q}
	\predDeltaU
	\Deltaub
	\\
	&+
	\Bar{\Tilde{\bm{e}}}^{T}
	\bm{Q}
	\Bar{\Tilde{\bm{e}}}.
\end{align*}

Due to $	\Bar{\Tilde{\bm{e}}}^{T}\bm{Q} \Bar{\Tilde{\bm{e}}}$ being constant in relation to the optimization vector, it can be ignored. Using the aforementioned compact notation and coefficient comparison for the matrices of the Quadratic Program \eqref{equ:2_QP} can be conducted.

\begin{align*}
	J &=  \quad \| {\Deltaub} \|_{\left( \predDeltaU \right)^\T  \bm{Q} \predDeltaU + \bm{R}}^2 &+\quad& 2 \left(\Tilde{\Bar{\bm{e}}}[k+1]\right)^\T \bm{Q} \predDeltaU \Deltaub \\
	 &\overset{!}{=} \quad \frac{1}{2} \| {\Deltaub} \|_{\bm{\Gamma}}^2  &+\quad&  \bm{\gamma}^\T \Deltaub \\
\end{align*}

Keeping the factor $1/2$ for the quadratic term in mind, the matrices $\bm \Gamma, \bm \gamma$ are defined as

\subsection{Voltage Constraints} \label{sec:mpcc_opt_v}
As mentioned in Section \ref{sec:intro_vsi}, the stator voltage vector $\bm{u}_S^S$ is subject to the constraints of the voltage hexagon spanned by the 6 active vectors. This is depicted in Figure \ref{fig:2_SRF_hexagon}.

\begin{figure}[h]
	\centering
	\include{tikz/SRF_hexagon.tex}
	\caption{Voltage Hexagon in SRF}
	\label{fig:2_SRF_hexagon}
\end{figure}

These constraints can be formulated as a set of linear inequality constraints. For example, the upper flat border of the hexagon is described by 
\begin{equation}
	u_\beta \le \frac{U_{DC}}{\sqrt{3}}
	\label{equ:constr_Nu1}
\end{equation}
and the upper right border, with a slope of $-\pi/3$ by
\begin{equation}
	u_\beta \le -{\sqrt{3}} u_\alpha + \frac{2\;U_{DC}}{\sqrt{3}}
	\label{equ:constr_Nu2}
\end{equation}
Both of the inequalities \eqref{equ:constr_Nu1} and \eqref{equ:constr_Nu2} describe convex sets. Continuing this construction of the voltage hexagon for all six borders, leads to a sixfold convex intersection of half-planes as in \cite{LMIVHex} \cite{NeunerMA}, which describes the voltage hexagon. Combining those six relations leads to the set of Linear Inequalities in SRF \eqref{equ:constraint_U_SRF}.
\begin{equation}
	\scriptstyle 
	\underbrace{
		\begin{bmatrix}
			\sqrt{3} & 1 \\
			0 & 1 \\
			-\sqrt{3} & 1 \\
			-\sqrt{3} & -1 \\
			0 & -1 \\
			\sqrt{3} & -1
		\end{bmatrix}
	}_{\bm{N}_u^S}
	\begin{bmatrix}
		u_\alpha\\
		u_\beta
	\end{bmatrix}
	\le
	\underbrace{
		\dfrac{U_{DC}}{\sqrt{3}}
		\begin{bmatrix}
			2\\
			1\\

\[
\bm{N}_u^S{\bm{u}^S_S[k]} \le \bm{\nu^S_u}
\]
However, the optimization problem shall be defined with respect to the the rotor reference frame in terms of the incremental stator voltage predictions. To do so, first, the rotation from the RRF is applied to  $\bm{u}_S^R[k] = \bm{\Delta u}_S^R[k] + \bm{u}_S^R[k-1]$ for one  voltage vector.
\[
\bm{N}_u^S \ipark\left(\bm{\Delta u}_S^R[k] + \bm{u}_S^R[k-1] \right)\le \bm{\nu^S_u}
\]
The next step is to extend that formulation to the incremental prediction vector ${\bm{\Delta u}}_S^R[k] \rightarrow \bar{\bm{\Delta u}}_S^R[k]$. To calculate ${\bm{u}}_S^R[k+1] \dots {\bm{u}}_S^R[k+N_P-1]$ from ${\bm{u}}_S^R[k-1]$ and ${\bm{\Delta u}}_S^R[k+l]$, all the ${\bm{\Delta u}}_S^R[k+l]$ with $l \leq N_P-1$ must be accumulated. Thus, the prediction vector in RRF is multiplied by the lower block-triangular matrix $\bm{P}_T$ with $\mathbf{I}_2$ as block elements.
\[
\Bar{\bm{u}}[k] = \bm{P}_T \Deltaub +  (\bm{1}_{N_P} \otimes \bm{I}_2) \bm{u}[k-1]
\]
With $ (\bm{1}_{N_P} \otimes \bm{I}_2)$ being the Kronecker product, describing $N_P$ stacked identity matrices. Putting everything together leads to:
\begin{equation}
	\Bar{\bm{N}}_u^S \predipark\left(\bm{P}_T \bar{\bm{\Delta u}}_S^R[k] +  (\bm{1}_{N_P} \otimes \bm{I}_2)\bm{u}_S^R[k-1] \right)\le \Bar{\bm{\nu}}^S_u

\end{equation}

\subsection{Hildreth's Quadratic Programming Procedure}

The Quadratic Program \eqref{equ:2_QP} is solved using Hildreth's Quadratic Programming Procedure, as recommended in \cite{mpcWang}, due to its benefits for real-time optimization problems. The iterative solver employs the basis vectors as search directions, in contrast to the Newton approach, which determines a search direction based on the Hessian matrix of the cost function, $\nabla^2 J = \bm{\Gamma}$. Therefore Hildreth's procedure performs an element-by-element search, where only one Lagrange multiplier is updated at each step. \\
Some of the key advantages of Hildreth's procedure are:
\begin{description}
	\item[Solution improves monotonically]: If a feasable solution exists, successive iterations are guaranteed converge towards the optimum.
	\item[Fixed computational effort]: The number of iterations can be limited due the monotonic improvement.
	\item[Element by element search]: No matrix inversion is required. This reduces the computational burden.
\end{description}
For a detailed description of this procedure in MPC applications refer to the MPC Book by L. Wang \cite{mpcWang}.

\section{MPC Formulations}
For field-oriented current control applications, three different MPC formulations are compared. The first one, referred to here as the Basic MPC, is based on \cite{mpcCamacho} \cite{mpcRawlings} and is often used in the literature due to its straightforward implementation.\\
The second approach utilizes an augmented incremental state-space system in order to embed an integrator directly into the controller structure, as proposed in \cite{mpcWang}.\\
The third can be thought of as a hybrid approach combining the previous two formulations, in order to obtain an embedded integrator while retaining a similar implementation to the Basic MPC. As will be shown, this is beneficial for extensions, such as those described in Section \ref{sec:mpcc_ext}.\\
It is worth mentioning that, for LTI systems, so-called offset-free MPC can be used to guarantee the rejection of constant disturbances. In the classical approach this is achieved by estimating the disturbances as an additional state with a constant dynamic $\hat{d}[k+1] = \hat{d}[k]$. For reference, see chapter 5 of \cite{mpcRawlings} or the summary paper \cite{ofmpcBM} , which builds on the original work presented in \cite{ofmpcPGR}. However, since a nonlinear prediction model was chosen for this application, some of the key assumptions made in those publications cannot be satisfied. This problem was recognized and the approach presented in \cite{ofmpcDistObs} mitigates this by estimating multiple disturbances for the application of FOC for PMSM.  
\subsection{Basic MPC}\label{sec:mpcc_mpc_basic}
The prediction according to Equation \eqref{equ:2_prediction} uses absolute states $\x[k]$ and differential actuating signal $ \bm{\Delta u}[k] = \bm{u}[k]-\bm{u}[k-1] $. This enables the utilization of a commonly used MPC structure, as described in Section 2.3 of \cite{mpcCamacho}, which is depicted in Figure \ref{fig:OaC_MPC}. \\
\begin{figure}[h]
	\centering
	\include{tikz/OaC_MPC.tex}
	\caption{Basis MPC structure used in \cite{mpcCamacho} with the notation used in this thesis}
	\label{fig:OaC_MPC}
\end{figure}
The MPC is provided with a reference prediction $$\bar{\bm{r}}[k+1] = \left[ \bm{r}^\T[k+1]\; \bm{r}^\T[k+2] \; \hdots \bm{r}^\T[k+N_P] \right]^\T$$. Using current states and the previous input, the auxiliary prediction vector $\Bar{\bm{g} [k]}$ can be computed according to Equation \ref{equ:2_prediction}. Using $\Tilde{\Bar{\bm{e}}}[k+1] = \Bar{\bm{g}}[k] - \Bar{\bm{r}}[k+1]$ to calculate the linear term $\bm{\gamma}$ of the quadratic program, the cost function $J = \wLNorm{\Delta u}{\Gamma} + \bm{\gamma^T} \Deltaub$ is fully determined. To determine the constraints $\bm{M} \Deltaub \le \bm{\mu}$, measured state vector and previous input are required. The output of the Quadratic Program \eqref{equ:2_QP} is the incremental input $\Deltaub$, which is accumulated then  to $\bm{u}[k]$. The interface to the plant is modeled as Sample and Hold elements. \\
Section \ref{sec:mpcc_analysis} provides an analysis on why this approach does not inherently guarantee offset-free tracking, but can be extended  by using the approach discussed in Section \ref{sec:mpcc_ext_is}.
 
\subsection{MPC with Embedded Integrator}\label{sec:mpcc_mpc_wang}
A different approach is used by L. Wang \cite{mpcWang}. Here, the state space system is augmented, such that the states and actuating signals are incremental. As it will be shown in Section \ref{sec:mpcc_analysis}, this ensures the MPC is of integrating behavior for locally unconstrained operation. In this thesis a brief overview of the derivation of the augmented state space model is given. For more details, refer to Section 1.2 of the MPC Book by Wang \cite{mpcWang}.\\
For discrete-time, linear time-invariant systems, without feedthrough term $\Sigma: \left[\bm{A}, \bm{B}, \bm{C} \right]$, the incremental states and inputs are defined as:
\begin{align*}
\bm{\Delta x}[k]  = \x[k] - \x[k-1] & \quad & \bm{\Delta u}[k] = \bm{u}[k] - \bm{u}[k-1] 
\end{align*}
In order to predict the absolute state or output $\bm{y}$, an additional state is introduced that accumulates the incremental quantities. This leads to the following augmented state-space model:
\begin{equation}
	\begin{split}
			\begin{bmatrix}
				\bm{\Delta x}[k+1] \\
				\bm{y}[k+1]
			\end{bmatrix}
		&=
			\begin{bmatrix}
				\bm{A} & \bm{0} \\
				\bm{C}\bm{A} & \bm{I}
			\end{bmatrix}
			\begin{bmatrix}
				\bm{\Delta x}[k] \\
				\bm{y}[k]
			\end{bmatrix}
		+
			\begin{bmatrix}
				\bm{B} \\
				\bm{C}\bm{B}
			\end{bmatrix}
		\bm{\Delta u}[k]
		\\
		\bm{y}[k]
		&=
			\begin{bmatrix}
				\bm{0} & \bm{I}
			\end{bmatrix}
		\begin{bmatrix}
			\bm{\Delta x}[k] \\
			\bm{y}[k]
		\end{bmatrix}
	\end{split}
	\label{equ:2_augmented_wang}
\end{equation}

Applying this to the description of the affine, parameter-varying discrete time model used for the PMSM \eqref{equ:2_apv_tk}, one has to take especially care for the the $\omega$-varying terms:

\begin{align*}
	\Sigma_{k+1}&: \left[\bm{A}(\omega[k]),\bm{B},\bm{E}(\omega[k])\right] - \Sigma_k:  \left[\bm{A}(\omega[k-1]),\bm{B},\bm{E}(\omega[k-1])\right] = \\
	\x[k+1] - \x[k] &= \left( \Aw \left(\x[k-1] + \bm{\Delta x}[k] \right)+ \B \bm{u}[k] + \Ew  \right) \\
	& - \left(\bm{A}(\omega[k-1])\x[k-1] + \B \bm{u}[k-1] + \bm{E}(\omega[k-1]) \right) \\
	\bm{\Delta x}[k+1] &= \Aw \bm{\Delta x}[k] + \B \cdot \bm{\Delta u}[k] + \underbrace{\bm{\Delta A}(\Delta \omega[k]) \x[k-1] + \bm{\Delta E}(\Delta \omega[k])}_{\bm{F}[k]} \\
\end{align*}
with
\begin{align*}
	\bm{\Delta A}(\Delta \omega[k]) &=
	\begin{bmatrix} 
		\bm{0} & T_s\dfrac{L_q}{L_d}\Delta\omega[k] \\
		-T_s\dfrac{L_d}{L_q}\Delta\omega[k] & \bm{0}
	\end{bmatrix} 
	\quad
	\bm{\Delta E}(\Delta \omega[k]) = 
	 \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\
	 	-\frac{T_s\psi_{PM}}{L_q} \Delta \omega[k] 
	 \end{bmatrix}.
\end{align*}
Applying the incremental state accumulation as in Equation \eqref{equ:2_augmented_wang}, the augmented state space system for the PMSM can be derived:
\begin{equation}
	\begin{split}
		\underbrace{
			\begin{bmatrix}
				\bm{\Delta x}[k+1] \\
				\bm{y}[k+1]
			\end{bmatrix}
		}_{\bm{\xi}[k+1]}
		&=
		\underbrace{
			\begin{bmatrix}
				\Aw & \bm{0} \\
				\bm{C} \Aw & \bm{I}
			\end{bmatrix}
		}_{\bm{A}_{\Delta}(\omega[k])}
		\underbrace{
			\begin{bmatrix}
				\bm{\Delta x}[k] \\
				\bm{y}[k]
			\end{bmatrix}
		}_{\bm{\xi}[k]}
		+
		\underbrace{
			\begin{bmatrix}
				\bm{B} \\
				\bm{C}\bm{B}
			\end{bmatrix}
		}_{\bm{B}_{\Delta}}
		\bm{\Delta u}[k]
			+ 
		\underbrace{
			\begin{bmatrix}
				\bm{F} [k]  \\
				\bm{C} \bm{F} [k] 
		\end{bmatrix}
		}_{\bm{E}_{\Delta} [k]}
		\\
		\bm{y}[k]
		&=
		\underbrace{
			\begin{bmatrix}
				\bm{0} & \bm{I}
			\end{bmatrix}
		}_{\bm{C}_{\Delta}}
		\begin{bmatrix}
			\bm{\Delta x}[k] \\
			\bm{y}[k]
		\end{bmatrix}
	\end{split}
	\label{equ:2_augmented_pmsm}
\end{equation}

Apart from the ambiguity regarding prediction assumptions for ${{\Delta \omega}}$, implementing constraints or extensions (Section \ref{sec:mpcc_ext}) with incremental quantities for states, $\Aw$ and $\Ew$ proved to introduce additional complexity.\\

For the implementation of this augmented system, one has to obtain the incremental state change $\bm{\Delta x} [k] $. This is accomplished by the filter $1-z^{-1}$. To calculate the constraints and tracking error, $u[k-1]$ and $x[k]$ are needed. This results into the controller structure shown in Figure \ref{fig:Wang_MPC}.

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\include{tikz/Wang_MPC.tex}
	\caption{MPC structure used in \cite{mpcWang} with the notation used in this thesis}
	\label{fig:Wang_MPC}
\end{figure}
Where $ \mathcal{\bm P}_{\Delta x}$ is the prediction matrices for the incremental state feedback of the augmented system $\Sigma_\Delta$, similar to the structure of $\bm{P}_x$ as given by Equation \eqref{equ:2_prediction}. 
The advantage of this approach, which introduces integrating behavior, is accompanied by the additional complexity due incremental states for prediction.

\subsection{Hybrid Approach}\label{sec:mpcc_mpc_hybrid}
Since the basic MPC formulation does not inherently guarantee offset-free tracking under constant disturbances, such as model faults and the embedded integrator according to Wang hinders extensions and constraint formulations due to its incremental states, a way searched to combine the benefits of those two approaches. \\
To obtain an MPC using absolute states $\x[k] = [i_{d}\; i_{q}]^\T$ and still having no direct feedback for $\bm{u}[k-1]$ for the locally unconstrained controller, like in the Embedded Integrator structure of, a hybrid structure of these two approaches was designed. The result is depicted in Figure \ref{fig:Hybrid_MPC}.
\\
The idea behind this structure is to use the state description to estimate what input $\Tilde{{\bm{u}}}[k-1]$ was applied to the plant such that it leads to the measured states $x[k]$. By doing so, input disturbances $\bm{d}_1[k-1] = \Tilde{{\bm{u}}}[k-1] - {{\bm{u}}}[k-1]$ are considered. 
\begin{equation*}
	\begin{split}
		\bm{x}[k]
		&= \bm{A}\bm{x}[k-1]
		+ \bm{B}
		\underbrace{
			\left(
			\bm{d}_1[k-1] + \bm{u}[k-1]
			\right)
		}_{\tilde{\bm{u}}[k-1]}
		\\
		\tilde{\bm{u}}[k-1]
		&= \bm{B}^{-1}
		\left(
		\bm{x}[k] - \bm{A}\bm{x}[k-1]
		\right).
	\end{split}
\end{equation*}
\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\include{tikz/Hybrid_MPC.tex}
	\caption{Hybrid MPC structure for LTI systems}
	\label{fig:Hybrid_MPC}
\end{figure}
Generally speaking, the inverse $\bm{B}^{-1}$ is not guaranteed to exist. However, in the case of the PMSM model used \eqref{equ:2_apv_tk}, $\B$ is a diagonal matrix with positive elements. With $L_d,\; L_q$ being in the range of $[0.1, 10]$ mH, $\B$ is well conditioned.\\
For the affine, parameter-varying prediction model $\Ew$ has to be considered as well:
\[
\tilde{\bm{u}}[k-1] = \bm{B}^{-1} \left( \bm{x}[k] - \bm{A}(\omega[k-1])\bm{x}[k-1] - \bm{E}(\omega[k-1]) \right).
\]
The dynamic behavior of this approach will be analyzed and compared to the other two formulations in the Section \ref{sec:mpcc_analysis}.

\section{Analysis of the Controller Structure}\label{sec:mpcc_analysis}
The main objective of this Section is to analyze the effect of constant disturbances such as model faults on the reference tracking of the controller. Since for the scope of this thesis, the stator current relation of the PMSM according to Equation \eqref{equ:1_apv_ss}, will be the only plant considered it is deemed sufficient to only analyze the three different controller structures.

\subsection{Closed-Loop Structure with inactive Constraints}
For the unconstrained case, the  Quadratic Program can be solved offline by finding the stationary points of $J$ with respect to $\Deltaub$:

\begin{align*}
	\nabla_{\Deltaub} J &= \bm{\Gamma}\Deltaub + \bm{\gamma} = 0 \\
		{\bm{\Delta u}}^* [k] &= -\bm{\Gamma}^{-1} \bm{\gamma}
\end{align*}
With  the Hessian matrix $ \nabla_{\Deltaub}^2 J = \bm{\Gamma}  \succ 0 $ ensuring the stationary point to be a minimizer and $\bm{\Gamma}  \succ 0$ is ensured by the choice of $\bm{Q, R}$.\\

In model predictive control only the first signal of the computed optimal input prediction is applied, thus the locally unconstrained  linear control law $\Deltaub =\bm{K}{\Bar{\bm{e}}}[k])$ can be written as  Relation \eqref{equ:2_solve_uc_qp}.
\begin{align}
	\bm{\Delta u} [k] & = -[\bm{I}\; \bm{0} \hdots \bm{0}]\bm{\Gamma}^{-1} \bm{\gamma} \\
	&= \underbrace{-[\bm{I}\; \bm{0} \hdots \bm{0}] (\predDeltaU^T \bm{Q} \predDeltaU +  \bm{R}^{-1})\predDeltaU \bm{Q}}_{\bm{K}} \Bar{\Tilde{\bm{e}}}[k] \\
	{\bm{\Delta u}}[k] &= \bm{K} \Bar{\Tilde{\bm{e}}}[k] 
	\label{equ:2_solve_uc_qp}
\end{align}

\subsubsection{Unconstrained Basic MPC for LPV Systems}
To improve the readability of this section, the parameter dependencies in $\omega$ are not always explicitly stated.
\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\include{tikz/OaC_MPC_unconstrained.tex}
	\caption{Unconstrained Basic MPC structure for LPV systems}
	\label{fig:Basic_MPC_unconstrained}
\end{figure}
Inserting $\tilde{\bar{\bm e}}[k]=\bar{\bm r}[k+1]-\bm \bar{\bm{g}}[k]$, according to the structure depicted of Figure \ref{fig:Basic_MPC_unconstrained}, the Control Law \eqref{equ:2_solve_uc_qp} can be expanded to
\[
\Deltaub = \bm{K} \left(\bar{\bm{r}}[k+1] - \left( \bm{P}_u \bm{u}[k-1] + \bm{P}_x \x[k]\right) \right) 
\]
With the output accumulation $\bm{u}[k] = \bm{u}[k-1] + \Deltaub$ one derives:

\[
\bm{u}[k] =  \bm{u}[k-1] - \bm{K}\bm{P}_u \bm{u}[k-1] + \bm{K}  \bar{\bm{r}}[k+1] - \bm{K}\bm{P}_x \x[k]
\]

For that system, the measured states and the reference prediction vector act as input $\bm{v}_{C,b}[k] = \x[k]\;  \bar{\bm{r}}[k+1]]^\T$ to the controller and the stator voltage as output $\bm{y}_{C,b}[k] = \bm{u}[k]$. 

\begin{align}
	\bm{u}[k]
	&=
	\underbrace{
		\begin{bmatrix}
			\bm{I} -\bm{K}\bm{P}_u(\omega[k])
		\end{bmatrix}
	}_{\bm{A}_{C,b}}
	\underbrace{
	\bm{u}[k-1]}_{\bm{\xi}_{C,b}[k-1]}
	\quad+\quad
	\underbrace{
		\begin{bmatrix}
			\bm{K} & -\bm{K}\bm{P}_x(\omega[k])
		\end{bmatrix}
	}_{\bm{B}_{C,b}}
	\underbrace{
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\bar{\bm{r}}[k+1]\\
		\bm{x}[k]
	\end{bmatrix}}_{\bm{v}_{C,b}[k-1]}
	\\
	\bm{y}_{C,b}[k]
	&=
	\underbrace{
	\bm{A}_{C,b}}_{\bm{C}_{C,b}}
	\bm{\xi}_{C,b}[k-1]
	+
	\underbrace{ 
	\bm{B}_{C,b}}_{\bm{D}_{C,b}}
	\bm{v}_{C,b}[k-1]
	=
	\bm{u}[k].
	\label{equ:2_ss_basic_mpc}
\end{align}
The spectrum of the unconstrained Basic Controller's system matrix $\bm{A}_{C,b}(\omega)$, was quantitatively analyzed by plotting its eigenvalues for discrete values of $\omega \in \mathcal{W} = [-10^3, 10^3]$ rad/s.
The result can be seen in Figure \ref{fig:2_basic_mpc_spectrum}.

\bm{G}_{vy}\big|_{\omega_l} = \bm{C}_{C,b}\big|_{\omega_l}  \left(z \I - \bm{A}_{C,b}\big|_{\omega_l}  \right)^{-1}\bm{B}_{C,b} + \bm{D}_{C,b}

\subsubsection{Unconstrained Hybrid MPC for LPV Systems}
Again, to improve the readability of this section, the parameter dependencies $\omega$ are not explicitly stated.\\
The reconstructed plant input is defined by Equation \eqref{equ:2_mpcc_ukt}.
\begin{equation}
	\tilde{\bm{u}}[k-1] = \bm B^{-1} \left( \bm x[k]-\bm A\bm x[k-1] \right)
	\label{equ:2_mpcc_ukt}
\end{equation}
The control law is linear in terms of the quasi-tracking error with the optimal control gain $\bm{K}$ according to Equation \eqref{equ:2_solve_uc_qp} $\Delta\bm u[k]=\bm K\tilde{\bm e}[k]$. The accumulated plant input is then given by
\begin{equation}
	\bm u[k]=\bm u[k-1]+\bm K\tilde{\bm e}[k]
	\label{equ:2_hyb_u_accum}
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\include{tikz/Hybrid_MPC_unconstrained.tex}
	\caption{Unconstrained Hybrid MPC structure for LPV systems}
	\label{fig:Hybrid_MPC_unconstrained}
\end{figure}

Using $\tilde{\bar{\bm e}}[k]=\bar{\bm r}[k+1]-\bm \tilde{\bar{\bm{g}}}[k]$, with the definition of $\tilde{\bar{\bm{g}}}[k]$ according to the structure depicted in Figure \ref{fig:Hybrid_MPC_unconstrained}, Equation \eqref{equ:2_hyb_u_accum} can be expanded to:
\begin{align*}
	\bm u[k]
	&=
	\bm u[k-1]
	+\bm K
	\left(
	\bar{\bm r}[k+1]
	-\bm P_u
	\left(
	\bm B^{-1}
	\left(
	\bm x[k]-\bm A\bm x[k-1]
	\right)
	\right)
	-\bm P_x\bm x[k]
	\right)
	\\
	&=
	\bm u[k-1]
	+\bm K\bm P_u\bm B^{-1}\bm x[k-1]
	+\bm K\bar{\bm r}[k+1]
	-\bm K\bm P_u\bm B^{-1}\bm x[k]
	-\bm K\bm P_x\bm x[k].
\end{align*}

After introducing a shift register for $\x[k-1]$, this can be written in matrix notation

\begin{align}
		\begin{bmatrix}
		\bm u[k]\\
		\bm x[k]
	\end{bmatrix}
	=
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\bm I & \bm K\bm P_u\bm B^{-1}\bm A\\
		\bm 0 & \bm 0
	\end{bmatrix}
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\bm u[k-1]\\
		\bm x[k-1]
	\end{bmatrix}
	+
	\begin{bmatrix}
		-\bm K &
		\bm K\left(\bm P_u\bm B^{-1}+\bm P_x\right)
		\\
		\bm 0 & \bm I
	\end{bmatrix}
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\bar{\bm r}[k+1]\\
		\bm x[k]
	\end{bmatrix}
	\label{equ:2_hyb_mpc_ss}
\end{align}


For that system, the measured states and the reference $ \bm{v}_{C,h} = [\bar{\bm{r}}\;\x]^\T$ act as input to the controller and the stator voltage as output $\bm{y}_{C,h}[k] = \bm{u}[k]$. To derive a state space formulation the state vector is defined as the following $\bm{\xi_{C,h}}^T = [\bm{u}\; \bm{x}]$.
\begin{align}
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\bm{u}[k] \\\bm{x}[k]
	\end{bmatrix} &= 
	\underbrace{
		\begin{bmatrix}
			\bm{I} & -\bm{K} \bm{P}_u(\omega[k]) \bm{B}^{-1} \bm{A}(\omega[k-1]) \\
			\bm{0} & \bm{0}
	\end{bmatrix}}_{\bm{A}_{C,h}}
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\bm{u}[k-1] \\\bm{x}[k-1]
	\end{bmatrix} + \\ & \hspace{3cm}
	\underbrace{
		\begin{bmatrix}
			-\bm{K} & \bm{K}\left(\bm{P}_u(\omega[k]) \bm{B}^{-1} +  \bm{P}_x(\omega[k]) \right) \\
			 \bm{0} & \bm{I} 
	\end{bmatrix}}_{\bm{B}_{C,h}}
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\bar{\bm r}[k+1]\\
		\bm x[k]
	\end{bmatrix}
	\\
	\bm{y}_{C,h}[k] &= [\bm{I} \; \bm{0}] \cdot \bm{A}_{C,h} \cdot \bm{\xi}[k-1]+ [\bm{I} \; \bm{0}] \cdot \bm{B}_{C,h} \cdot  \bm{v}_{C,h}[k-1] = \bm{u}[k]
	\label{equ:2_ss_hyb_mpc}
\end{align}
According to the LPV State Space Model \eqref{equ:2_ss_hyb_mpc}, the system matrix $\bm{A}_{C,h}$ is an upper block-triangular matrix. Thus the eigenvalues are on the main diagonal of it. Since those entries are constant, the spectrum of this system matrix is given by
\begin{equation*}
\sigma \left( \bm{A}_{C,h} \left(\omega\right) \right) = \{1, 1, 0, 0\} 
\end{equation*}
With two discrete-time eigenvalues at one, it can be said, that this controller is of intergrating behavior. \\
Again, the transfer function matrix $\bm{v} \rightarrow \bm{y}$ for frozen parameter $\omega = \omega_l$ is derived, so that the frequency response can be analyzed. 

\[
 \bm{G}_{vy}\big|_{\omega_l} = \bm{C}_{C,h}\big|_{\omega_l}  \left(z \I - \bm{A}_{C,h}\big|_{\omega_l}  \right)^{-1}\bm{B}_{C,h} + \bm{D}_{C,h}
\]

The obtained frequency responses for $\omega \in [-1\; -0.5 \; 0 \; 0.5 \; 1] \cdot 10^3$ rad/s are depicted in Figure \ref{fig:2_hyb_mpc_mimo_tfs}.
\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{Figs/2_hyb_mpc_mimo_tfs}
	\caption{Discrete-time frequency response for the unconstrained Hybrid MPC.}
	\label{fig:2_hyb_mpc_mimo_tfs}
\end{figure}

The different frozen transfer functions are fairly similar, with their magnitude response differing in the sub-dB range. Thus, they appear to be nearly identical in Figure \ref{fig:2_hyb_mpc_mimo_tfs}.

\subsubsection{Unconstrained MPC with Embedded Integrator for LPV Systems}

If the notation used in this thesis is applied to Equation (1.29) of Wang's Book \cite{mpcWang} for the optimal control signal of the linear unconstrained augmented system $\Sigma_\Delta: \left(\bm{A}_\Delta, \bm{B}_\Delta, \bm{C}_\Delta,\right)$ \eqref{equ:2_augmented_wang}, the result is given by Equation \eqref{equ:2_wang_Deltaub_opt}. Where $\{ \mathcal{\bm P}_{\Delta u}, \mathcal{\bm P}_{\Delta x} \}$ are the prediction matrices for the state feedback and input of the augmented system $\Sigma_\Delta$, similar to the structure of $\{\predDeltaU, \bm{P}_x\}$ as given by Equation \eqref{equ:2_prediction}. 

\begin{align}
	\Deltaub^*
	&= [\bm{I}\; \bm{0} \hdots \bm{0}]
	\left(
	\mathcal{\bm P}_{\Delta u}^\T
	\mathcal{\bm P}_{\Delta u}
	+ \bm{R}
	\right)^{-1}
	\left(
	\mathcal{\bm P}_{\Delta u}^\T
	\mathcal{\bm P}_{r} \bm{r}[k+1]
	-
	\mathcal{\bm P}_{\Delta u}^\T
	\mathcal{\bm P}_{\Delta x}
	\underbrace{[\Delta \x[k]\; \bm{y}[k]]}_{\bm{\xi}_{\Delta}[k]}
	\right)
	\label{equ:2_wang_Deltaub_opt}
	\\
	&= \bm{K}_y
	\left(
	\bm{r}[k+1] - \bm{y}[k]
	\right)
	+ \bm{K}_x \Delta \x[k]
	\label{equ:2_wang_KxKy}
\end{align}

By doing a coefficient comparison with of Equations \eqref{equ:2_wang_Deltaub_opt} and \eqref{equ:2_wang_KxKy}, the linear control gains can be determined. With that the Diagram \ref{fig:Wang_MPC_unconstrained} for the locally unconstrained controller can be derived.

	\centering

Using the same method for the derivation of the state space model for the Basic and Hybrid MPC, the dynamics for the unconstrained embedded integrator MPC formulation is derived.

\begin{equation}
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\bm{u}[k] \\ \bm{x}[k]
	\end{bmatrix}
	= 
	\underbrace{
		\begin{bmatrix}
			\bm{I} & \bm{K}_x \\ \bm{0} & \bm{0}
	\end{bmatrix}}_{\bm{A}_{C,ei}} 
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\bm{u}[k-1] \\ \bm{x}[k-1]
	\end{bmatrix} 
	+
	\underbrace{
		\begin{bmatrix}
			-\bm{K}_y & \bm{K}_y - \bm{K}_x \\

	\begin{bmatrix}
		\bm{r}[k+1]\\

	\end{bmatrix}

\end{equation}

Again, the controller's system matrix $\bm{A}_{C,ei}$ of Equation \eqref{equ:2_ss_ei_mpc} an upper block-triangular. Thus, its spectrum can be easily written down:

\[
\sigma \left( \bm{A}_{C,ei} \left(\omega\right) \right) = \{1, 1, 0, 0\}.
\]

With that the integrating behavior in the unconstrained operation can be directly observed.

\subsection{Comparison of the MPC Formulations with inactive Constraints}
The Basic MPC stores the last control signal $\bm{u}[k-1]$ and uses it directly for the prediction. This results directly in feedback structure of the accumulator $z/(z-1)$ as seen in Figure \ref{fig:Basic_MPC_unconstrained}. As confirmed by the eigenvalue and transfer function analysis this results in low-pass behavior. Therefore this approach does not guarantee offset-free tracking under constant disturbances such as model faults. Section \ref{sec:mpcc_ext_is} introduces an extension of this formulation for integral behavior. However, as it will be shown, this extension requires anti-windup measures for implementation on real hardware.\\
Using an augmented incremental state space system  $\Sigma_\Delta: \left(\bm{A}_\Delta, \bm{B}_\Delta, \bm{C}_\Delta,\right)$ according to Wang \cite{mpcWang} the accumulator is in direct series connection to the input of the plant. However, the augmented state space system $\Sigma_\Delta$ with the augmented state vector ${\bm{\xi}_{\Delta}[k]} = [\Delta \x[k]\; \bm{y}[k]]^\T$ hinders the flexibility of this formulation for extensions, such as the ones presented in the following Section.\\
To ensure integral behavior and use absolute states $\bm{x}$ the direct feedback of the accumulator was interrupted by estimating the previous control signal $\tilde{\bm{u}}[k-1] = \bm{B}^{-1}\left(\bm{x}[k] - \bm{A}\bm{x}[k-1] \right)$ which can be thought of as the actual last control signal disturbed by an input disturbance $\tilde{\bm{u}}[k-1]  = \bm{u} [k-1] + \bm{d}_1[k-1]$. This formulation is untypical, since it requires $\bm{B}^{-1}$ which is in general not guaranteed to exist, but poses no issues for the model of the PMSM $\eqref{equ:2_apv_tk}$. As seen by the transfer functions of Figure \ref{fig:2_hyb_mpc_mimo_tfs} and the spectrum of the controller dynamic matrix, this leads to integral action. With that, offset free tracking under constant disturbances is ensured.\\
If the MPC operates with active constraints, the high frequency dynamics might be worth studying, to gain insight if one of those three presented approaches tends to less robust behavior with respect to model and timing uncertainties. For that the Bode Plots \ref{fig:2_basic_mpc_mimo_tfs} and \ref{fig:2_basic_mpc_mimo_tfs} are consulted. Looking at high frequencies within the sampling limit $ \qty{5}{kHz} \le f <  T_S^{-1}/2$, the magnitude attenuation of the Hybrid formulation is close to identical to the Basic formulation for $\bm{r} \rightarrow \bm{u}$, but the Basic MPC exhibits 15 dB more attenuation for all frozen transfer functions of state feedback to actuating signal $\bm{x} \rightarrow \bm{u}$ than the Hybrid MPC. 

\section{Extensions} \label{sec:mpcc_ext}
Three extensions for the model-predictive field oriented control of PMSM where chosen. First the issue of computation and switching delay is addressed with deadtime compensation. Section \ref{sec:ext_deadt_comp} describes the well-established approach for one-step compensation. Chapter \ref{ch:ipred} explores methods to compensate for fractional delays.\\
Since the Basic MPC formulation according to \cite{mpcCamacho} does not compensate for e.g. model faults, an extension is presented which solves this by considering the integral tracking error in the cost function.\\
Finally, to allow for short-term violations of current limits, soft constraints are introduced.

\subsection{Deadtime Compensation} \label{sec:ext_deadt_comp}
For clarity, the prediction notation $u[i|j]$ is introduced, where $i, j \in \mathbb{Z}$. Here, $i$ denotes the time of application or measurement of the variable, and $j$ denotes the time of information acquisition or computation. Thus, for example, $u[k+1|k]$ is the control variable calculated at time $k$, which takes effect starting at $k+1$.\\

Due to the one-step input delay of the computation of the PWM's duty cycle, the control variable $u[k+1|k]$ calculated at time $k$ does not influence the transition from $k$ to $k+1$, but rather the transition from $k+1$ to $k+2$. Therefore, the MPC prediction does not begin with $\hat{y}[k+1|k]$, but with $\hat{y}[k+2|k]$. The starting point is the known intermediate prediction based on $\SigmaApvTk$.

\begin{equation}
	\hat{\bm{x}}[k+1|k] = \Aw \bm{x}[k|k] + \bm{B}\bm{u}[k|k-1] + \Ew
	\label{equ:delComp_sys_model}
\end{equation}

where $\boldsymbol{u}[k|k-1]$ is the control variable calculated in the previous sampling step and now in effect. The optimization variable thus starts at $\Delta \boldsymbol{u}[k+1|k]$, and the first predicted output influenced by it is $\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}[k+2|k].$\ \

Delay compensation affects both the control variable calculation and the transformation from the rotor-fixed to the stator-fixed coordinate system $dq \rightarrow \alpha\beta$. Due to the equidistant, PWM-synchronous sampling, the pre-calculated angle
\[
\hat{\varphi}_e[k+1|k] = \varphi_e[k|k] + T_S \omega_e[k|k]
\]
is used instead for the transformations between SRF and RRF.\\

At time $k$, because $\boldsymbol{C}=\boldsymbol{I}$, the output $ \boldsymbol{y}[k|k]=\boldsymbol{x}[k|k] $ is measured. Based on this, the MPC calculates the output sequence
\[
\left\{\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}[k+2|k],\dots,\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}[k+N_P|k]\right\}
\]
for the control input sequence
\[
\left\{\Delta \boldsymbol{u}[k+1|k],\dots,\Delta \boldsymbol{u}[k+N_C|k]\right\}
\]
and determines $\boldsymbol{u}[k+1|k]$ from this by minimizing the cost function \eqref{equ:2_J}. The measurement result $\boldsymbol{y}[k+1|k+1]$ is still determined by the control input $\boldsymbol{u}[k|k-1]$ that is already active; the newly calculated control input $\boldsymbol{u}[k+1|k]$ takes effect only in the following sampling interval.

\subsubsection{Implementation}
Without deadtime compensation, the objective of the quadratic program is to determine the prediction vector $\Deltaub$. This vector is composed of:

\begin{align*}
	\Bar{ \boldsymbol{\Delta u}}^*[k] = \begin{bmatrix}
		{ \boldsymbol{\Delta u}}^*[k] \\
		{ \boldsymbol{\Delta u}}^*[k+1]\\
		\vdots\\
		{ \boldsymbol{\Delta u}}^*[k+N_C-1]
	\end{bmatrix}
	= 
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\Delta u_d^*[k] \\
		\Delta u_q^*[k] \\
		%\hdashline
		\Delta u_d^*[k+1] \\
		\Delta u_q^*[k+1] \\
		%\hdashline 
		\vdots  \\
		%\hdashline 
		\Delta u_d^*[k+N_C-1] \\
		\Delta u_q^*[k+N_C-1] 
	\end{bmatrix}
\end{align*}

To compensate the deadtime, the optimization target must be 
\begin{align*}
	\Bar{ \boldsymbol{\Delta u}}^*[k+1|k] = \begin{bmatrix}
		{ \boldsymbol{\Delta u}}^*[k+1|k]\\
		\vdots\\
		{ \boldsymbol{\Delta u}}^*[k+N_C-1|k]
	\end{bmatrix}
\end{align*}.

with that, the new cost function is 

\begin{equation}
	J_{\mathrm{dtc}}
	=
	\left\|
	\Bar{\boldsymbol{y}}[k+2]
	-
	\Bar{\boldsymbol{r}}[k+2]
	\right\|_{\bm{Q}}^2
	+
	\left\|
	\Bar{\boldsymbol{\Delta u}}[k+1|k]
	\right\|_{\bm{R}}^2
	\label{equ:delComp_J_compact}
\end{equation}

To achieve this, the prediction equation \eqref{equ:2_prediction} must be modified according to:

\begin{equation}
%	\Bar{\bm{y}}[k+2|k]
%	=
%	\underbrace{
%		\Tilde{\bm{P}}_{x}\Bar{\bm{x}}[k]
%		+
%		\Tilde{\bm{P}}_{u}\bm{u}[k|k-1]
%		+
%		\Tilde{\bm{P}}_{E}  \Ew
%	}_{=:\Bar{\Tilde{\bm{g}}}[k+1|k]}
%	+
%	\Tilde{\bm{P}}_{\Bar{\bm{\Delta u}}}
%	\Bar{\bm{\Delta u}}[k+1|k]
\Bar{\boldsymbol{y}}[k+2|k] = \underbrace{ \Tilde{\boldsymbol{P}}_{x} \hat{\boldsymbol{x}}[k+1|k]
	+ \Tilde{\boldsymbol{P}}_u \boldsymbol{u}[k|k-1] + \Tilde{\boldsymbol{P}}_{E}\Bar{\boldsymbol{E}}(\hat{\omega}[k+1|k]) }_{=:\Bar{\boldsymbol{g}}[k+1|k]} + \Tilde{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\Bar{\Delta u}} {\Bar{\boldsymbol{\Delta u}}}[k+1|k] 
	\label{equ:2_prediction_dtc}
\end{equation}

Or in more detail:

\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
		&\underbrace{
			\begin{bmatrix}
				\hat{\bm{y}}[k+2] \\
				\vdots \\
				\hat{\bm{y}}[k+D_s]
			\end{bmatrix}
		}_{\Bar{\bm{y}}[k+2|k]}
		=
		\underbrace{
			\begin{bmatrix}
				\bm{C}\left(\Aw\right)^2 \\
				\vdots \\
				\bm{C}\left(\Aw\right)^{D_s}
			\end{bmatrix}
		}_{\Tilde{\bm{P}}_x}
		\Bar{\bm{x}}[k]
		+
		\underbrace{
			\begin{bmatrix}
				\bm{C}\left(\Aw+\I\right)\B \\
				\vdots \\
				\bm{C}\left(\displaystyle\sum_{l=0}^{D_s-1}
				\left(\Aw\right)^l\right)\B
			\end{bmatrix}
		}_{\Tilde{\bm{P}}_u}
		\bm{u}[k|k-1]
		\\[1ex]
		&\quad+
		\underbrace{

The main idea is to calculate the accumulated tracking error with a designated dynamical system $\Sigma_z$ for each sampling step.
\[
\Sigma_z : \bm z[k+1] = \bm z[k] + (\bm y[k] - \bm r[k])
\]
Based on this system, the prediction for $\bm z[k+1]$ over $D_S$ is calculated. This prediction vector is then used as an additional quadratic term, weighted by an diagonal matrix $\bm{W}_I$ in the cost function, to minimize the integral error.
\begin{equation}
	J_{is} = \dots + \wLNorm{\bar{z}}{W_I}%(\Bar{\bm z}[k+1])^T W_I (\Bar{z}[k+1])
	\label{equ:2_Jis}
\end{equation}
 To directly use this in the Quadratic Program \eqref{equ:2_QP}, the prediction vector $\bar{\bm{z}}[k+1]$ is expressed in terms of $\Deltaub$. Using a similar procedure as described in Section \ref{sec:prediction_horizon}, the prediction vector for $\bm z$ is calculated by the recursive predictions
 \begin{align*}
 	\bm z[k+1]
 	&=
 	\bm z[k] + \bm y[k] - \bm r[k],\\
 	\bm z[k+2]
 	&=
 	\bm z[k] + \left(\bm y[k]-\bm r[k]\right)
 	+ \left(\bm y[k+1]-\bm r[k+1]\right),\\
 	&\;\vdots\\
 	\bm z[k+N_P]
 	&=
 	\bm z[k]
 	+
 	\sum_{l=0}^{N_P-1}
 	\left(\bm y[k+l]-\bm r[k+l]\right).
 \end{align*}. 
 Using matrix notation this can be written as
 \[
 \Bar{\bm z}[k+1]	=
 (\bm{1}_{N_P}\otimes\bm{I}_2)\bm{z}[k]
 +
 \bm{P}_T
 \left(
 \bar{\bm{y}}[k]
 -
 \Bar{\bm r}[k+1]
 \right)
 \]
 where $P_T$ is lower triangular block matrix consisting of identity matrices as elements.
 Substituting the Output Prediction \eqref{equ:2_prediction},
 \[
 \bar{\bm{y}}[k+1]
 =
 \bar{\bm{g}}[k]
 +
 \bm{P}_{\Delta u}\Deltaub
 \]
 into the recursive predictions above, the stacked integral-state
 prediction becomes
 \begin{align*}
 	\Bar{\bm z}[k+1]
 	&=
 	(\bm{1}_{N_P}\otimes\bm{I}_2)\bm{z}[k]
 	+
 	\bm{P}_T
 	\left(
 	\bar{\bm{g}}[k]
 	+
 	\bm{P}_{\Delta u}\Deltaub
 	-
 	\Bar{\bm r}[k+1]
 	\right)\\
 	&=
 	\underbrace{
 		(\bm{1}_{N_P}\otimes\bm{I}_2)\bm{z}[k]
 		+
 		\bm{P}_T
 		\left(
 		\bar{\bm{g}}[k]-\Bar{\bm r}[k+1]
 		\right)
 	}_{:=\,\bm{\rho}_I\text{, known at instance }k}
 	+
 	\underbrace{
 		\bm{P}_T\bm{P}_{\Delta u}
 	}_{:=\,\bm{P}_I\text{, related to }\Deltaub}
 	\Deltaub.
 \end{align*}
Due to the separation of the term $\bm{\rho}_I$ and the $\bm{P}_I$, $\Bar{\bm z}[k+1] = \bm{\rho}_I + \bm{P}_I  \Deltaub$, can easily be inserted into the modfied cost function $J_{is}$ of Equation \eqref{equ:2_Jis}.
\[
J_{is} = \| \bm{\Bar{y}}  - \bm{\Bar{r}} \|_{\bm Q}^2  + \wLNorm{\Bar{\boldsymbol{\Delta u}}}{R} + \| \bm{\rho}_I + \bm{P}_I  \Deltaub \|_{\bm{W}_I}^2
\]



Defining the auxiliary terms
\begin{align*}
	\bm{Q}_I 	  &= \bm{P}_I^\T \bm{W}_I \bm{P}_I 		\\
	\bm{\gamma}_I^\T &= \bm{\gamma}^\T + 2 \bm{\rho}_I^\T \bm{W}_I \bm{P}_I\\
	\bm{\Gamma}_I  &= \predDeltaU^\T (\bm{Q}  + \bm{Q}_I) \predDeltaU + \bm{R}
 \end{align*} 
eases the augmentation such that the quadratic program with integral states can be expressed as

\begin{equation}
	\begin{split}
		&\arg \min_{\Bar{\bm{\Delta  u}_S}}\;  \frac{1}{2} \| {\Deltaub} \|_{{\bm{\Gamma}}_I}^2 + \Tilde{\bm{\gamma}_I}^\T \Deltaub \\
		\text{s.t.} & \quad 
		{\bm{M}} \Deltaub\le {\bm{\mu}}
		\label{equ:2_QP_is}
	\end{split}
\end{equation}

The weighting matrix $\bm{W}_I$ is a tune-able factor. For that simulation, an ideal inverter was used, $\omega_m = 0$ rad/s and the simulated model mismatch of the MPC is with respect to the motor parameters of Table \ref{tab:params_NW3}. \subsubsection{Remarks}
Using this approach, integral action of the MPC can be ensured, without increasing the elements of optimization variables or sacrificing the Basic MPC formulation. However, if the MPC operates under active constraints and the tracking error cannot be fully compensated, the implicit integral error state $z$ will wind up. Looking at the cost function,

\[
J_{is} = \| \bm{\Bar{y}}  - \bm{\Bar{r}} \|_{\bm Q}^2  + \wLNorm{\Bar{\boldsymbol{\Delta u}}}{R} + \| \bm{z} \|_{\bm{W}_I}^2
\]
one can see that the quadratic form regarding $z$ will also grow in value.The solver of the quadratic program will still search for the vector $\Deltaub$ which minimizes that cost. Even though the effect of the weighting $\bm{Q,R}$ will still be considered, by that, numerical issues may arise due to that wind up effect.

\subsection{Soft Constraints} \label{sec:mpcc_ext_sc}
By adding a slackness variable $\eta_i$ to the left hand side of a linear inequality according to Equation \ref{equ:2_sc}
\begin{equation}
	m_i \zeta_i \le \mu_i + \eta_i
	\label{equ:2_sc}
\end{equation}
where $m_i, \mu_i$ are scalar constants, the optimization variable $\zeta_i$ can violate this constraint. For every step in time, the constraint slackness $m_i \zeta_i[k] - \mu_i = \eta_i[k]$ is then minimized by an extra term in the cost function with a weighting factor $w_\eta$. This results in a mechanism, which allows for short-term constraint violations, due to the modification of Equation \eqref{equ:2_sc}, but the linear term $\eta {w_\eta}$ of $J$, minimizes the slackness.\\
The vector  of optimization variables is extended by the slackness variable  $\boldsymbol{\Bar{\Delta u}} \rightarrow \bm{\zeta} = \begin{bmatrix}
	\bm{\Bar{\Delta u}}^\T & \eta
\end{bmatrix}^\T$. To reduce the number of elements in $\bm{\zeta}$, it was decided to use one scalar slackness variable, which is scaled by the constant vector $ \bm{\rho} \; : \; \rho_i \in \mathbb{R},\; 0 \le \rho_i \le 1$. The linear inequalities of the Quadratic Program \eqref{equ:2_QP} are extended to 
\[
\bm{M} \bm{\zeta} \le \bm{\mu} + \bm{ \rho} \eta
\].
%Using the compact L2-Norm Notation:
%\[
%\boldsymbol{\Bar{\Delta u}^\T}[k] \boldsymbol{Q} \boldsymbol{\Bar{\Delta u}}[k] = 
%\| \Bar{\boldsymbol{\Delta u}}\|_{\boldsymbol{Q}}^2
%\]
Considering the slackness variable and integral error states, the total cost function is then defined by
\begin{equation*}
	\Tilde{J}_\mathrm{tot} = \wLNorm{\Bar{y} - \Bar{r}}{Q}  + \wLNorm{\Bar{\Delta u}}{R} + \wLNorm{\Bar{z}}{W_I} + w_{\eta} \eta
\end{equation*}.

With that, the total modified Quadratic Program \eqref{equ:2_QP_sc} can be written down.

\begin{equation}
	\begin{split}
		\boldsymbol{\Bar{\Delta u}}^* [k] = 
		\underbrace{
		\footnotesize{\begin{bmatrix} 
				1 & 0 & & 0\\
				0& \ddots & &  \vdots\\
				& 0& 1 & 0
		\end{bmatrix}}}_{\left[\bm{I}^{mD_S \times mD_S}\;\vdots\;\bm{0}^{ mDs \times 1} \right]} 
		&\arg \min_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}
		\bm{\zeta}^T \begin{bmatrix}
			\Tilde{\bm{\Gamma}} & \bm{0} \\
			\bm{0}^T & w_{\eta}
		\end{bmatrix} \bm{\zeta} +
		\begin{bmatrix}
			{\bm{\gamma}}^T & 0
		\end{bmatrix} \bm{\zeta}
		\\
		\text{s.t. }& \begin{bmatrix}
			\bm{M} & -\bm{\rho} \\
			\bm{0}^T & 0
		\end{bmatrix} 
		\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}
				\Bar{\bm{\Delta u}}\\ \eta
		\end{bmatrix}}_{\bm{\zeta}}
		\leq \begin{bmatrix}
			{\bm{\mu}} \\ /
		\end{bmatrix}
	\end{split}
	\label{equ:2_QP_sc}
\end{equation}
The matrix $\left[\bm{I}^{mD_S \times mD_S}\;\vdots\;\bm{0}^{mD_S\times 1} \right]$ is used in order for $\Deltaub$ to be the optimization target (without $\eta$). In the implementation $[\bm{I}_2\; \bm{0}_2 \; \hdots\; \bm{0}_2 \; 0 ]$ instead of $\left[\bm{I}\;\vdots\;\bm{0} \right]$, such that only the first voltage vector $\bm{\Delta u}[k]$ is applied.
